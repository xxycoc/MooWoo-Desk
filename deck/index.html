The question

When a stock suddenly jumps or drops, is there a reason?

On 19 Nov, Woodward (WWD) rose 8% on three times its usual volume. The reason was in that morning's news: earnings came in 13% above forecasts.

Some big moves have a clear reason. Others don't. We bet that the difference matters.

Analyst Reads the news and rates the move

Saying no

The desk has rules for saying no, and it used them.

If this happensThe desk does this
The market is weak (S&P 500 below its recent trend) Blocks new news trades. Blocked Disney and Target on 19 Nov and PDD on 23 Nov.
The account loses 1.5% in a day No new buys until tomorrow.
The account is down 2%, 4% or 6% from its peak Smaller trades, then sells only, then sell everything (the kill switch).
A trade rises by a normal day's move Lock in profit with a stop that follows the price up.
A trade goes nowhere for 5 days Sell it.

When to stop

We wrote down in advance what would prove the idea wrong.

The idea fails if

After 15 closed news trades, the analyst's picks do worse than simply following every move, or simply fading every move.

Then news trades stop for the rest of the window, money moves to the steady funds, and we report the idea as failed.

We closed only one news trade, so this test never ran.

Hard limits on every order

  • At most 20% of the account in one stock
  • At most 60% in news trades, 40% when the market is weak
  • At least 10% kept in cash
  • Price spread at most 0.5%; prices no older than 60 seconds
  • Missing data can never approve a buy
  • Real-money order tools are switched off for every agent

What broke: day one

On the first day, 13 attempts produced zero trades.

Too many agents argued (bull, bear, manager and more) and passed long text to each other.

Answers came back as essays instead of clean data.

We cut it to one analyst giving short, strict answers. The next day the desk traded.

14 / 21

What broke: the brakes

The desk stopped itself six times. The last time, it cost us the final sell-off.

  1. 18 Nov

    The broker named a price field differently from what we expected.

  2. 18 Nov

    Cash and holdings were read seconds apart, prices moved, and the totals were $9.75 apart.

  3. 18 Nov

    A copied broker record lost a field.

  4. 20 Nov

    After a part-sale, the broker's average cost differed from ours.

  5. 25 Nov

    A sell order filled in two parts and looked like a mismatch.

  6. 27 Nov

    One quiet ETF's price was 106 seconds old. The 60-second check stopped the final sell-off twice, and the last retry reached the calendar after the close. Nine holdings were never sold.

No bad trade was made, but the last stop meant no final sale. For the first five: find the cause, fix it, write it down, add a test. Then a person restarted the desk.

A desk that stops when unsure is safer than one that guesses with money.

15 / 21

Lessons

What we would do differently.

  1. 1Start with one agent and one full day, then add more.
  2. 2Treat every broker reply as possibly wrong.
  3. 3Build the audit trail before the trading logic.
  4. 4Test the last day on its own. It only had three chances to work.

Reuse it

One skill up close: never trust broker data unchecked.

Why it exists

On 18 Nov the coordinator dropped a field while copying a broker record, and the desk stopped.

What it does

Keeps only the fields we need, checks them, and saves the file in one step. A bad reply is fetched again up to twice, then the run stops with a reason.

Why it is reusable

Any agent that reads a broker or an API can follow the same steps: never copy a summary, never guess a field name.

Agent templates

Each agent is two files: agent.json (model and tools) and system.md (its instructions), in agents/.

permissions.json switches off real-money order tools for every agent.

Only the executor can send paper orders. The auditor can only read.
